AKA Art Productions

Made with the artist, by hand.

Since a first exploration in Peru in 2007, we have travelled with contemporary artists to the places where a technique is still alive — Ecuador, Kyrgyzstan, Nepal, Mali, India, Uzbekistan — and produced the work there, by hand, with the master craftspeople who keep it.

00Peru and Ecuador · 2007–2018

Before the studio: reed, paper and straw.

The exploration began in Peru in 2007, among the reed boats of the coast — caballitos de totora, built by hand the same way for centuries.

In 2018, in Ecuador, Vargas-Suárez Universal spent days drawing on paper by the sea at Punta Barandúa, and in Manabí we went to the weavers of paja toquilla, the fine straw of the Panama hat. Each trip asked the same question: what can an artist make here, by hand, that could not be made anywhere else?

01Kyrgyzstan · 2018

Bishkek: an artist’s drawing, pressed into felt.

In September 2018 we made our first trip to Kyrgyzstan, to the felt-makers of ala-kiyiz and shyrdak — wool laid, wetted, rolled and pressed by hand, one of the oldest textile techniques in Central Asia. It was where AKA’s work with artists truly began.

The first project was with Vargas-Suárez Universal: his drawings were laid out full size on the workshop table, and a master felt-maker built them up in black and white wool, sample by sample, until the felt could hold every line.

02New York · 2019

The work comes to New York.

In 2019 the textile works we produced with Vargas-Suárez Universal were shown in New York, in an exhibition curated by Carla Stellweg in her own space: large pieces hung wall to wall, in the rooms where people live with art. One of them went on to hang in the double-height lobby of a new building in Manhattan.

The same year we visited Saint Clair Cemin’s studio in Brooklyn and began a textile collaboration with him.

See the full chapter · 4 photos

03Kyrgyzstan · 2019

Back to the felt: Cemin, Bedia, Burgos, Vargas-Suárez.

In 2019 we went back to Kyrgyzstan with new drawings. The felt-makers built them in ala-kiyiz — loose wool laid by colour on the table, then wetted, rolled and pressed until it binds into a single cloth.

For Saint Clair Cemin, a figure in blue, yellow and black taken from his painting on paper. For José Bedia, his drawings — Semana Santa, En el tronco de un árbol — in black wool, every line finished by hand. For Fabian Burgos, his bands of colour, each stripe felted in a gradient and pieced into the whole. For Vargas-Suárez Universal, the clouded blue of the work that later hung in New York.

Back in Miami, Burgos laid the finished felt beside his prints to compare them, and José Bedia received his pieces in his studio. In 2020 Bedia’s felts were exhibited, Semana Santa among them.

04Nepal · 2019–2021

Kathmandu, 2019: we start filming.

Lokta paper is made in the Himalayan foothills from the inner bark of the lokta bush. In 2019 we brought artists to Kathmandu and began filming their projects: Aya Takano in Tibetan hand-knotting, Jessica Stockholder in lokta paper. Jessica Stockholder made her own sheets and worked into them — tearing, layering, adding colour — learning the paper as she went. Vargas-Suárez Universal joined us for a work on paper. Aya Takano’s drawing went onto the loom, knot by knot, with our weavers.

Stockholder’s works were later shown by her galleries; Takano’s series was shown in Paris, Tokyo and Hong Kong. In 2021, hand-knotted tapestries we produced in Kathmandu for Carlos Amorales and Dr. Lakra were shown by kurimanzutto in East Hampton.

05Miami · December 2019

From Kathmandu to NADA Miami.

That December, the works came to Miami. At NADA Miami 2019, AKA Art Productions showed Jessica Stockholder’s lokta works — ten sheets made by her own hand in Kathmandu — on one wall of the booth.

Around them, on the walls and on the floor, the hand-knotted and felt works we had produced with the artists of the previous two years.

06Nepal · 2020

New works on the looms.

In 2020 three new projects went onto the looms in Nepal: Carlos Amorales’ black butterflies, Pedro Friedeberg’s rows of symbols, and Alexandre Arrechea’s works — among them a hand and a circle, each cut to its own shape. After the knotting, every piece is carved by hand: the pile is trimmed around each contour so the drawing stands up from the surface, then the edges are bound.

Jessica Stockholder’s textile work advanced on the same floors, and she kept experimenting in lokta. That year we also staged a photo shoot in Bhaktapur with By Kind People — lokta paper, hand-knotted rugs and woven shawls worn and carried through the old city — to bring Nepal’s local crafts to a wider public.

07New York and Nepal · 2021

2021: Latinx Abstract, and back to Nepal to film.

In 2021 a work by Vargas-Suárez Universal made with the felt-makers of Kyrgyzstan was shown in Latinx Abstract, curated by Elisabeth Ferrer, in New York.

The same year we went back to Nepal to film Lokta Kingdom: at the drying fields above Kathmandu at dawn, in the workshops where the paper is bound, and up into the hills where the lokta bush grows — by jeep along mountain roads and on foot to Ghorepani and Poon Hill, under Annapurna.

08Georgia · October 2021

Latinx Nomads: filming in Tbilisi and Mtskheta.

In October 2021 we took the camera to Georgia to film Latinx Nomads: six Latinx influencers discovering the country’s art and culture, with interviews with a former president of Georgia and Tekuna Gachechiladze, the country’s best-known chef, with several restaurants.

We filmed in Tbilisi; at Mtskheta, the old capital above the meeting of two rivers; and in the vaulted cellar of one of Georgia’s leading wineries.

09Tokyo · Hong Kong · Mexico City · 2021–2022

The works travel.

In autumn 2021 Aya Takano showed the works we made with her in Nepal at Perrotin Tokyo, and in 2022 at Perrotin Hong Kong.

In 2022 José Bedia’s felts from Kyrgyzstan were exhibited again; Pedro Friedeberg’s hand-knotted work from Nepal lay at the centre of his booth at Zona Maco, Mexico City; and Dr. Lakra’s first tapestry with us was embroidered in silk and wool.

102022–2024

Silk, wool and felt, at the scale of painting.

Dr. Lakra’s tapestry was embroidered in silk and wool in 2022: a red deity of chains and teeth over a river, every surface filled with chain stitch. Felt followed — a dragon above a skeleton, laid in wool.

In 2023 José Bedia’s works were on view again, and Aya Takano’s hand-knotted works from Nepal went up on gallery walls. In 2024 Edgar Orlanieta’s triptych was made in felt: a cross, a dove and a sun, drawn in wool and stitched by hand.

12Kyrgyzstan → Art Basel · 2022–2024

Carlos Amorales, in felt.

Four years after the first trip, the felt-makers made Carlos Amorales’ first piece in Kyrgyz felt, in May 2022. The series that followed brings ala-kiyiz and shyrdak — pressed and cut wool — to the scale of a painting.

Jungle Star (Universe), 289 × 203 cm, was shown by kurimanzutto at Art Basel Miami Beach, 4–8 December 2024. Other works in the series have been shown at international art fairs.

13Mali · 2024

Bogolan: cloth painted with mud.

Bogolanfini is Mali’s mud cloth: cotton dyed with plants, then painted, line by line, with fermented mud. With artisans in Mali we translated Carlos Amorales’ forms into the technique.

Dreamachine, cotton painted with mud, 160 × 120 cm, was shown at Albarrán Bourdais, Madrid, in 2024.

14India and Nepal · 2024–2025

Mother of pearl, hair and black stone.

In Udaipur, craftspeople cut mother of pearl and abalone into small tiles and set them one by one on a drawn grid. With Gabriel de la Mora we produced mother-of-pearl works there, and hanging works in human hair, 90 × 60 cm; his human-hair works were shown at Museo Jumex.

In Nepal, stone carvers cut Dr. Lakra’s drawing — a monkey, a skull between two cobras, a crocodile on a lotus base — in black ballistic stone, working from the artist’s drawings and proportion guides, with revisions sent between studio and workshop.

See the full chapter · 4 photos

15Kathmandu · 2025

Dr. Lakra, carved in black stone in Kathmandu.

Dr. Lakra drew a column of creatures: a monkey on a coiled cap, a skull held between two hooded cobras, a ringed spine, and a crocodile curled around a lotus base. We took the drawing to stone carvers in Kathmandu and had it cut, by hand, in black ballistic stone.

Each part was roughed out of its own block with hammer and chisel, the artist’s printed drawing on the floor beside the carver, then refined, fitted and finished. Revisions travelled between the studio and the workshop until every scale and ring matched the drawing.

16Uzbekistan · 2025–2026

Tejido Líquido: twelve metres of silk, by hand.

Silk was always the goal. From 2018 we wrote to, called and visited more than twenty-five groups of artisans, looking for someone able to embroider silk at the scale we had in mind. In 2022 we found the workshop of Bahodir and his daughter Aisha in Uzbekistan, who weave their own cloth and raise their own silkworms.

For Carlos Amorales we produced Tejido Líquido: a suzani of 1.80 × 12 m, embroidered in bigiz saima, the hook stitch of the Fergana Valley, with hand-dyed silk on silk-and-cotton cloth woven by the workshop. The black is built up from successive indigo baths. In the collections and sources we consulted, we have not found a suzani of comparable size.

How a production works

The artist leads. We translate the drawing into a technique and a workshop.

  1. 1ProposalThe artist’s drawing and intention, size and use.
  2. 2Technique & workshopWe match the work to the craft and to the master craftspeople who keep it alive.
  3. 3SamplesColour, material and density approved by the artist before production.
  4. 4ProductionMade by hand in the workshop, with documentation for each piece.
  5. 5DeliveryFinishing, shipping and delivery handled end to end.
